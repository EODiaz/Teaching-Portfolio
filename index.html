About This Portfolio

This portfolio collects the course materials I developed during the Interdisciplinary Teaching seminar in UCF's Writing & Rhetoric graduate program (Fall 2026). The central project is a designed section of ENC 1101, From Page to Screen: The Art of Adaptation, which uses the relationship between literary texts and their screen adaptations as both the subject of inquiry and the vehicle for developing students as analytical writers and critical thinkers.

Alongside that course design, I have developed a signature assignment, an assessment rubric, an AI policy, and a teaching statement that together reflect my pedagogical commitments: to student choice, iterative revision, critical media literacy, and honest engagement with the tools and conditions that shape contemporary intellectual life.

Teaching Philosophy

“The most important questions about any text — who made this, for whom, and to what end — do not respect departmental boundaries, and neither should the methods we teach students to answer them.”

My teaching centers on a simple premise: students read more carefully when they care about what they are reading, and they write more honestly when they have genuine stakes in the argument. In my AICE English Language and Media Studies courses at South Miami Senior High, I build from texts students already encounter and ask harder questions about them. At the college level, that same instinct drives the design of From Page to Screen, where students choose their own source texts and their own mode of inquiry — because genuine choice is not an accommodation, it is a theory of motivation made concrete.

The digital humanities dimension of my practice is embedded in the questions I ask, not layered on top. When students analyze a film poster or a streaming interface alongside a literary text, they are already examining how form shapes meaning, how platform conditions reception, how authorship is distributed. My goal is to make that work visible and nameable — and to send students out of the course with habits of inquiry that work on any text, in any medium.

Approach to AI

My course AI policy emerged from firsthand experimentation rather than institutional guidance. Using AI tools to perform the same textual and visual analysis I ask students to perform — and noting precisely where the AI succeeded and where it failed — produced a clearer account of the tool's limitations than any policy document could. The result is an approach built around three commitments:

Three Principles

  • AI as object of study, not just a tool. Students are invited to use AI as a thinking partner and planning aid, and are required to document where the tool fell short and what their own thinking added.
  • Transparency over prohibition. Every assignment requires an AI Use Statement that names the tools used, the specific uses, and at least one concrete limitation or bias encountered in the AI's engagement with the material.
  • Honest reckoning with cost. These tools were built through labor chains that include underpaid data workers, and their operation carries real environmental costs. A course that asks who made this and at what cost applies that question to its own tools.
